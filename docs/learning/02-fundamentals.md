# MODULE 2: FUNDAMENTAL ANALYSIS

## Overview

Fundamental analysis is how professional investors evaluate whether a stock is a good investment. You'll learn to read financial statements, understand key metrics like P/E ratios and profit margins, and compare companies to identify winners.

**Duration:** ~25 minutes
**Prerequisites:** Module 1 (Basics)
**Level:** Beginner-Intermediate

## What is Fundamental Analysis?

**Fundamental analysis** means evaluating a company's financial health and business prospects to determine if its stock is fairly priced.

**The Core Question:** Is this company making money, growing, and worth the current stock price?

**What You'll Analyze:**
1. Financial statements (income, balance sheet, cash flow)
2. Profitability metrics (margins, ROE, ROA)
3. Growth rates (revenue, earnings)
4. Peer comparison (is it better than competitors?)

## The Three Financial Statements

Every public company publishes three key financial statements quarterly and annually:

### 1. Income Statement (Profit & Loss)

Shows revenue, expenses, and profit over a period (quarter or year).

**Key Lines:**
- **Revenue** (Top Line): Total money from selling products/services
- **Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)**: Direct costs to make products
- **Gross Profit**: Revenue - COGS
- **Operating Expenses**: Marketing, R&D, admin costs
- **Operating Income (EBIT)**: Profit from core business
- **Net Income** (Bottom Line): Profit after all expenses and taxes

**Example - Apple Q4 2024 (Simplified):**
```
Revenue:               $90 billion
Cost of Goods Sold:    -$52 billion
Gross Profit:          $38 billion (42% margin)
Operating Expenses:    -$14 billion
Operating Income:      $24 billion
Taxes:                 -$4 billion
Net Income:            $20 billion
```

**Key Insight:** Apple keeps $20B of every $90B in revenue - that's a 22% net margin (excellent!).

### 2. Balance Sheet (Assets vs Liabilities)

Shows what the company owns (assets) and owes (liabilities) at a point in time.

**Formula:** Assets = Liabilities + Equity

**Key Components:**
- **Assets:** Cash, inventory, property, equipment
- **Liabilities:** Debt, accounts payable, obligations
- **Equity:** What's left for shareholders (assets - liabilities)

**Example - Tesla (Simplified):**
```
Assets:
  Cash:                 $26 billion
  Inventory:            $13 billion
  Property/Equipment:   $28 billion
  Total Assets:         $107 billion

Liabilities:
  Debt:                 $5 billion
  Accounts Payable:     $18 billion
  Total Liabilities:    $43 billion

Shareholder Equity:     $64 billion
```

**Key Insight:** Tesla has more assets than liabilities (healthy balance sheet) and lots of cash ($26B).

### 3. Cash Flow Statement

Shows actual cash moving in and out. Companies can be "profitable" on paper but still run out of cash!

**Three Sections:**
- **Operating Cash Flow (OCF):** Cash from business operations
- **Investing Cash Flow:** Cash spent on equipment, acquisitions
- **Financing Cash Flow:** Cash from borrowing or paying dividends

**Why It Matters:** A company with negative cash flow might struggle to pay bills, even if showing profits.

**Example:**
```
Operating Cash Flow:    +$25 billion (GOOD - generating cash)
Investing Cash Flow:    -$10 billion (buying equipment - normal)
Financing Cash Flow:    -$5 billion (paying down debt - good)
Net Cash Change:        +$10 billion (cash position improved)
```

## Critical Financial Metrics

### Revenue (Top Line)

Total money from sales before any expenses.

**Why It Matters:** Growing revenue = growing business. But revenue alone doesn't mean profit!

**Example:**
- Apple 2023 Revenue: $383 billion
- Apple 2024 Revenue: $391 billion
- **Growth: +2.1% YoY** (Year-over-Year)

Look for steady revenue growth (5-15%+ annually for healthy companies).

### Earnings Per Share (EPS)

Profit divided by number of shares.

**Formula:** EPS = Net Income / Shares Outstanding

**Example:**
- Microsoft net income: $72 billion
- Shares outstanding: 7.4 billion
- **EPS: $9.72**

**Why It Matters:** EPS growth = shareholders getting richer. Stock prices often follow EPS trends.

### Profit Margins

Margins show how much profit a company keeps from each dollar of revenue.

#### Gross Margin

**Formula:** (Revenue - COGS) / Revenue × 100

**Example:**
- Apple Revenue: $90B, COGS: $52B
- Gross Margin: ($90B - $52B) / $90B = **42.2%**

**What's Good?**
- Software companies: 70-90% (low marginal costs)
- Hardware companies: 20-40%
- Retailers: 10-30%

#### Operating Margin

**Formula:** Operating Income / Revenue × 100

Shows profit from core business before interest/taxes.

**Example:**
- Apple Operating Income: $24B, Revenue: $90B
- Operating Margin: $24B / $90B = **26.7%**

#### Net Margin

**Formula:** Net Income / Revenue × 100

Bottom line - profit after ALL expenses.

**Example:**
- Apple Net Income: $20B, Revenue: $90B
- Net Margin: $20B / $90B = **22.2%**

**Industry Benchmarks:**
- Tech: 15-25% (high)
- Consumer goods: 5-10% (moderate)
- Retail: 2-5% (low)
- Airlines: 0-5% (very low)

### Return on Equity (ROE)

How efficiently does the company turn shareholder money into profit?

**Formula:** ROE = Net Income / Shareholder Equity × 100

**Example:**
- Company Net Income: $10B
- Shareholder Equity: $50B
- **ROE: 20%**

**What's Good?**
- >15%: Excellent
- 10-15%: Good
- <10%: Mediocre
- <0%: Losing money

**Interpretation:** For every $1 of shareholder equity, the company generates $0.20 in profit.

### Debt-to-Equity Ratio

How much debt vs equity does the company use?

**Formula:** Total Debt / Shareholder Equity

**Example:**
- Company Debt: $20B
- Equity: $50B
- **D/E Ratio: 0.4**

**What's Good?**
- <0.5: Conservative (low debt)
- 0.5-1.0: Moderate
- >1.0: High leverage (risky)
- >2.0: Very risky

**Context Matters:** Utilities and banks often have high D/E (normal for their industries). Tech companies typically have low D/E.

### Free Cash Flow (FCF)

Cash left after paying expenses and capital expenditures.

**Formula:** FCF = Operating Cash Flow - Capital Expenditures

**Example:**
- Operating Cash Flow: $30B
- CapEx (equipment spending): $8B
- **FCF: $22B**

**Why It Matters:** FCF can fund dividends, buybacks, debt repayment, or growth. Companies with strong FCF have financial flexibility.

## Key Valuation Metrics

### P/E Ratio (Price-to-Earnings)

Most widely used valuation metric.

**Formula:** P/E = Stock Price / Earnings Per Share

**Example:**
- Apple stock: $225
- Apple EPS: $7.50
- **P/E: 30x**

**Interpretation:** Investors pay $30 for every $1 of Apple's earnings.

**What's Good?**
- S&P 500 average: 15-20x
- Growth stocks: 25-50x+ (expecting rapid growth)
- Value stocks: 8-15x (mature, slower growth)

**Compare within industries!** Tech P/E of 30x might be normal, but 30x for a utility would be expensive.

### P/S Ratio (Price-to-Sales)

Stock price relative to revenue (useful for companies with no profits yet).

**Formula:** P/S = Market Cap / Annual Revenue

**Example:**
- Tesla Market Cap: $800B
- Tesla Revenue: $95B
- **P/S: 8.4x**

**What's Good?**
- <1x: Potentially undervalued
- 1-2x: Fair value (mature companies)
- >5x: Premium valuation (growth expectations)

### P/B Ratio (Price-to-Book)

Stock price relative to book value (assets minus liabilities).

**Formula:** P/B = Stock Price / Book Value Per Share

**Example:**
- Bank stock: $50
- Book value per share: $40
- **P/B: 1.25x**

**What's Good?**
- <1x: Trading below book value (potentially undervalued)
- 1-3x: Reasonable
- >5x: Premium (often for high-growth or asset-light businesses)

**Best for:** Banks, real estate, manufacturing (asset-heavy businesses)

## Comparing Companies (Peer Analysis)

Never evaluate a company in isolation. Compare to competitors!

**Example: Comparing Tech Giants**

```
Company    | P/E  | Revenue Growth | Net Margin | FCF
-----------|------|----------------|------------|--------
Apple      | 28x  | +2%            | 22%        | $95B
Microsoft  | 32x  | +13%           | 36%        | $60B
Google     | 24x  | +9%            | 24%        | $65B
```

**Insights:**
- Microsoft has highest P/E → Investors expect strong growth (justified by 13% revenue growth)
- Apple has highest FCF → Mature cash cow
- Microsoft has best margins → Most efficient operations

## Reading Earnings Reports

Public companies report earnings quarterly. These reports move stock prices!

**What to Look For:**

### 1. Beat or Miss?

Compare actual results to analyst expectations.

**Example:**
- Expected EPS: $1.25
- Actual EPS: $1.35
- **Beat by $0.10 (8%)** → Stock likely rises

### 2. Revenue Growth

Is revenue increasing quarter-over-quarter and year-over-year?

**Example:**
- Q4 2023: $90B
- Q4 2024: $95B
- **+5.6% YoY growth** → Healthy

### 3. Guidance

What does management expect next quarter?

**Example:**
"We expect Q1 2025 revenue of $85-88 billion, representing 3-7% growth."

If guidance is below expectations, stock may fall even if current quarter was good!

### 4. Key Metrics

Look for metrics specific to the business:
- **Tech:** User growth, cloud revenue, subscription revenue
- **Retail:** Same-store sales, comparable sales growth
- **Manufacturing:** Orders backlog, capacity utilization

## Qualitative Factors

Numbers aren't everything! Also consider:

### Competitive Advantages ("Moats")

What protects this company from competition?

**Types of Moats:**
- **Brand power** (Apple, Coca-Cola)
- **Network effects** (Facebook, Visa)
- **Cost advantages** (Walmart's scale)
- **Patents/IP** (Pharmaceutical companies)
- **Switching costs** (enterprise software)

**Example:** Apple's brand loyalty means customers pay premium prices and rarely switch to competitors.

### Management Quality

Good CEO? Track record of execution? Honest communication?

**Red Flags:**
- Frequent accounting restatements
- High executive turnover
- Overly optimistic guidance repeatedly missed
- Excessive CEO compensation

### Industry Trends

Is the industry growing or declining?

**Examples:**
- ✅ Cloud computing: Growing 20%+ annually (good for Microsoft, Amazon)
- ❌ Traditional retail: Declining (challenging for mall-based stores)

## Common Mistakes

### ❌ Mistake 1: Only Looking at P/E

P/E alone doesn't tell the full story. A low P/E might mean "cheap" or might mean "company has problems."

**Solution:** Use multiple metrics (P/E, margins, growth, FCF).

### ❌ Mistake 2: Ignoring Industry Context

A P/E of 15x is expensive for a bank, cheap for a tech company.

**Solution:** Always compare to industry peers.

### ❌ Mistake 3: Focusing Only on Past Performance

Historical growth doesn't guarantee future growth.

**Solution:** Look at forward guidance and industry trends.

### ❌ Mistake 4: Overlooking Balance Sheet

A profitable company with too much debt can still go bankrupt.

**Solution:** Check debt levels and cash reserves.

## Key Takeaways

✅ **Financial statements** show revenue, profits, assets, liabilities, and cash flow
✅ **EPS** (earnings per share) is profit per share - track its growth
✅ **Margins** (gross, operating, net) show profitability efficiency
✅ **P/E ratio** compares stock price to earnings - lower isn't always better
✅ **Compare to peers** - context matters for every metric
✅ **ROE >15%** indicates efficient use of shareholder money
✅ **Free cash flow** shows financial flexibility
✅ **Qualitative factors** (moats, management, trends) matter as much as numbers
